A two-player Ludo night on a video call: fewer captures, more chat

Ahmed Moaz · 17 October 2026

Ludo with two people is a different game from Ludo with four. It is quieter, with far fewer captures, and it is luckier. That makes it a good game to have running beside a long video call, where the talk matters more than the board.

Why two seats are calmer

The difference is in how often someone gets sent home. I build Boardit, a browser game site, and on 29 September 2026 its live rules engine played 30,024 games of Ludo between computer players, to see how the game behaves. In the 10,008 two-player games, the average was 4.4 captures a game, about two each. At four players it was 30.0. And 3.5% of two-player games had no capture at all.

The typical simulated two-player game had 4 captures, and about one in ten reached 8 or more. So a capture at two seats is an event. At four it is the weather.

The same simulation can't tell you what a human pair will do. Computer players don't hold back to be kind, and most of these were set up to take a capture whenever one was there.

Setting up beside the call

Keep the call in one window and the board in another, on the same screen or a second device. Boardit has no voice or video of its own, so the call does the talking.

Each throw is followed by one move, and the only choice is which piece makes it. A six, a capture or a piece reaching home earns another throw, so a turn can run on, but three sixes in a row lose it. That suits a conversation: you can glance at the board, throw, move, and pick up the sentence you were in.

What changes when there's only one opponent

With one opponent, fewer pieces can threaten yours. Boardit's guides say a capture is usually worth taking against one opponent and often not at a full table. That is advice, not a measurement, but the reason is simple: only one person can hit back.

The same thinness makes the game luckier. Boardit's two-player page puts it plainly: at two players Ludo "tilts further toward luck than at four". Boardit's page adds that a better player wins more often, not always, so any single game can go to the luckier thrower.

A position to think through

The Ludo page on Boardit, the site I build, sets out the safe squares and the capture rules that the advice above turns on. Ludo is still mostly luck.

When the connection drops

Online, the thing to plan for is a dropped connection. The turn clock does not wait for you. When your 30 seconds run out, the table plays an obvious move: a capture if there is one, else bringing a piece home, else bringing one out on a six, else moving your furthest piece. Boardit's video-call page warns that it "may not be the one you wanted".

When the game was tested in August with two browsers on the live site, a capture showed on both screens and a chat message got through. That's the part a call needs.

What two seats can't give you

Two-player Ludo online follows the same Ludo rules as a full board, which makes it an easy way to play Ludo with friends on a call.

You need the other person, because Boardit has nobody to play against except people. Two of the board's four corners sit empty. And the numbers above are simulated, not a record of real couples playing.

Written by Ahmed Moaz. Figures labelled as simulated come from computer play, not from people.
