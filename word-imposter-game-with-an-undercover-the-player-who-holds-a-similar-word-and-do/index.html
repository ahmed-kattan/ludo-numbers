Word imposter game with an Undercover: the player who holds a similar word and does not know it

Ahmed Moaz · 27 October 2026

Most word imposter game rounds have one question at the centre: who is the spy? The Undercover option adds a second, stranger question. What if somebody at the table is certain they know the word, and is wrong? In Who's the Spy it is a switch the host can turn on before the match. It is off by default.

What is the Undercover?

One extra player each round is dealt a word that is close to the table's word. Nothing on their screen marks them as different, so they cannot tell that their word is the odd one. They give clues in good faith, and the clues sound almost right.

Take two made-up words. The table holds "snerf", a heavy purple lantern that hums. The Undercover holds "snarf", a heavy purple lantern that whistles. A clue like "heavy and purple" suits both. A clue like "it hums" suits only the table's word.

The spy is a separate role. The spy still knows only the category.

How big must the table be?

The Undercover only appears when at least three players besides the spies are seated. With one spy, that means four players or more. At three players the round has only two civilians once the spy is counted, so the game skips the Undercover and plays a normal round.

A table of six with one spy and the Undercover on has four players who share the word, one who holds the near-miss, and one who holds nothing. That is a lot of fog, so I would leave it off for a first game.

How does it change the clues?

Everyone who holds the real word now has two kinds of suspect. One will say things that are too general. The other will say things that are a little wrong. A good civilian listens for both.

For the Undercover, the pressure is odd. You have no idea you are different. All you notice is that somebody else's clue felt slightly off. The right move is to give clues you truly believe, and trust that the table will sort it out.

For the spy, the Undercover can be a gift or a trap. Their wrong-but-confident clues draw suspicion away from you. But if you copy them, you copy the wrong word.

What does the Undercover score?

The rules for points stay simple.

  • Naming a spy still earns the voter a point, as before, and naming the Undercover earns one too.
  • An Undercover who escapes the vote scores a point, like a spy who slips through.
  • The Undercover's own vote earns nothing.
  • If the table votes out the Undercover, the real spy has slipped through and scores.

The last round's Undercover is not picked again if there is anyone else to pick. When the round ends, the Undercover's word is revealed next to the table's, so everyone learns what the near-miss was.

Is the Undercover game worth switching on?

It suits tables who already know the basics. Once everyone can write a clue in 20 characters, the extra role adds a second layer of reading, and rounds feel fresher.

It does not suit a table of four who are still learning the rules. With one spy and one Undercover, half the players hold something unusual, and the civilians' job becomes guesswork.

To try it out, Boardit's Who's the Spy offers the Undercover as an option in the lobby, and it also works as a plain spy game online without it.

Why do both roles belong in one game?

They test different skills. The spy is a performer, hiding a gap. The Undercover is a victim of circumstance, with a gap they cannot see. Putting both in one round gives you a bluffer and an honest mistake, and telling them apart is the fun.

Starting small

Play one plain game first. Then start a second game with the Undercover switched on in the lobby, and see whether anyone notices the difference before the vote.
