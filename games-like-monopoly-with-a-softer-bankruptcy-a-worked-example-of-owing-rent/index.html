Games like Monopoly with a softer bankruptcy: a worked example of owing rent

Ahmed Moaz · 28 October 2026

The knock-out is the part of a property game that people remember with a wince. One rent lands, you cannot pay it, and you are finished while the others play on. Plenty of the people who search for games like Monopoly want that moment softened. I build a property trading game where it is, so here is a worked example with real figures.

How does owing rent work?

Four sentences cover it.

  1. If you owe more rent than you hold, you hand over everything you have.
  2. The rest becomes a debt, recorded against the player who is owed.
  3. While you are below zero you cannot roll the dice, but you can manage your cities at any time, even when it is not your turn.
  4. You leave the game only when you declare bankruptcy, and that cannot be undone.

The third point matters most. The player in trouble is busy, not benched.

What does it look like with numbers?

Say you land on Alexandria, which a rival has built up with three houses. The rent is 270. You hold 100.

You pay the 100. You now owe 170. Your cash shows minus 170, you cannot roll, and your creditor will be paid as you raise money, not all at once.

Your options are the usual ones: mortgage a city, sell a building, or trade. Suppose you own Rome, Milan and Nice, with no buildings on them. Each mortgage pays half the price.

Rome and Milan together raise 150. That leaves you 20 short, so Nice goes too. In all you raise 240, you clear the 170, and you are left holding 70. Your rival has been paid as each sum came in, so they are not left waiting.

What does it cost to get out?

Not nothing. A mortgaged city earns nothing, and lifting the mortgage costs what you raised plus a tenth, rounded up. Rome costs 88, Milan 77 and Nice 99, which is 264 for the three. You raised 240, so the debt cost 24 extra and some time without rent.

That is why I call it softer, not soft. You stay in, but you pay for the privilege.

What if there is nothing to sell?

Then the rule delays, and does not rescue. A player with no cities, no buildings and no cash will end up declaring bankruptcy. The extra time is useful only if you have something to bargain with. A trade might still bring cash in. I do not want to pretend that a debt turns a lost game into a won one.

A sensible piece of advice follows from this. Mortgage the cities that earn you least first, and keep the ones that complete a country for as long as you can.

How do you stay out of debt in the first place?

Keep a cushion. Before you roll, look at the largest rent on the board that you could land on. Three houses on Alexandria charge 270. A hotel there charges 550. If your cash is below the rent you fear most, you are one throw from a mortgage.

A debt can also be owed to the bank, or to the Vacation pot, when the charge comes from a card or a fee and not from a rival. The same rules apply: you pay what you can and raise the rest.

Can the host make it harsher?

Yes. Mortgaging is a switch in the lobby. With it off, a player in debt can only sell buildings or trade, which makes debt far more dangerous. I would turn it off only for a table that wants the old, sharp knock-out back.

Who pays when a rival goes bankrupt?

A creditor is paid as the debtor raises money, so the order matters. Money raised first goes to what is owed first. This avoids a situation where a creditor is credited the whole sum at once, then the debtor leaves and the money was never there. The code used to credit the whole sum at once, and I changed it for that reason.
