Is there a snakes and ladders game strategy? What you can and cannot control

Ahmed Moaz · 18 October 2026

If you search for snakes and ladders online strategy tips, you will find plenty of confident advice. Most of it is wrong, and the reason is simple: in the standard snakes and ladders game, a player makes no decisions at all. You throw the die and move the counter that many squares. There is nothing to optimise.

So can anything improve your chances?

Not once the game has started. The die is the whole engine, and it is the same for everyone. The ladders and snakes are fixed on the board. The only moves are forced.

That is a feature, not a flaw. Because there is no strategy, a four-year-old can beat a grandparent, and neither feels cheated.

What can you choose before the game?

There are four real choices, and they all happen before the first throw:

  1. The number of players. More players mean a shorter game in rounds, because the first to finish ends it. Two players need around 26 rounds; eight need about 15.
  2. Where you sit. The first seat has a small edge: 50.8% in a game for two, 13.4% at a table of eight, where an even split would be 12.5%. You cannot change it, but you can swap who starts in each game.
  3. The finish rule. Insisting on an exact throw to land on 100 adds roughly three and a half throws to the journey. Agreeing a bounce-back or an overshoot win shortens it.
  4. The clock. A time limit per turn keeps everyone moving.

What can you control during the game?

Only the way you play it. You can:

  • keep the table lively by commenting on moves;
  • help younger players count squares;
  • make sure the right counter is moved;
  • make the game end on time by agreeing a cut-off.

These are social skills, not tactical ones. They matter more than any trick with the die.

Are there tricks that people believe in?

Yes, and none of them work:

  • Throwing the die softly or hard. On a printed board, a hard throw does not change the odds. Online, the die is rolled for you.
  • Counting out loud. It helps accuracy, not luck.
  • Lucky counters. Colour makes no difference to the throw.

If someone claims to have a system, ask them to write it down and test it over twenty games. It is a good small experiment for a family.

Is it still worth playing a no-decision game?

Absolutely. It is a game for sharing a moment, not for proving skill. A family game night needs a game that can end with laughter regardless of who wins, and this is one of the few that does so reliably. Pair it with a game that has decisions, and each evening has both kinds of fun.

The cost of the exact finish

For a purist, the biggest rule choice is the end of the game. The exact-finish rule costs 3.4 throws over a journey that averages 39. If you want to keep the evening short, drop it, but say so before the first throw.

A final word on advice

Be wary of anyone who sells a system for a game of pure chance. The honest advice is to enjoy the die, count your moves out loud, and congratulate whoever wins.
