Whose screen to share? Playing Ludo on a video call

Ahmed Moaz · 14 October 2026

Don't share one screen and call it Ludo. Put each player on their own device, keep the call for faces and voice, and share a screen only to show the board to guests who have no seat. The reason is the turn clock: every Ludo turn has 30 seconds, and the clock does not wait for a slow screen share.

That is the short answer for Ludo with friends on a video call. Ludo online suits a call well, and the timer is the one thing to plan around. The rest is how to choose when your group does not fit that neatly.

A Ludo table seats two to four, and each seat is one player on one device. That rules out the idea of one person playing all four colours while everyone shouts instructions. It also means a call of six cannot all play.

Why the 30-second clock decides it

Ludo has a fixed clock of 30 seconds a turn. When the clock hits zero, the table throws for you and plays an obvious move. A full lap of four players, each using the whole clock, takes 4 × 30 = 120 seconds, so a table that dawdles will feel slow.

Now add a household that plays as a team. The speaker has to hear the group, agree on a piece and tap, all inside 30 seconds. If the clock has to act for the same seat three times running, that player is out, about a minute and a half of nobody acting. A household with a lively debate can trip that rule.

The same goes for a screen share. Whoever watches a shared picture sees the board a moment after the player does. That is harmless for guests, who cannot act anyway, and a real handicap for anyone trying to decide from it.

What to do with the fifth, sixth and seventh guest

Let them watch. A Ludo host can allow up to 20 watchers, who see the board read-only and cannot move. Everyone else can keep chatting on the call, and nobody has to share a screen at all.

A player who gets all four pieces home can also leave the table while the rest play for the remaining places. On a call that is useful. The winner steps out of the table, stays on the call and starts heckling.

A rule of thumb

Count the people. Two to four who each have a device: one device each, and nothing shared. More than four: the first four play, the rest watch. One device in the room for the whole household: share nothing, and pick a speaker who is allowed to decide fast.

Before the first throw, have everyone read the Ludo rules once, so the arguments happen before the clock starts. Settle early whether a third six in a row really wastes the turn; that is the argument that eats call time.
