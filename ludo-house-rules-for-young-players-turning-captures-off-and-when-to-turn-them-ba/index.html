Ludo house rules for young players: turning captures off, and when to turn them back on

Ahmed Moaz · 6 November 2026

Few things sour a young player on ludo faster than watching a long walk get undone. One landing, and a piece that took many turns to bring round goes back to the corner. Among the ludo house rules I offer, the captures switch is the one for that moment. First, who this is for. The terms of Boardit say it is not meant for anyone under 13, so by "young" I mean teenagers new to the game, or a mixed family table where the older players want the youngest to enjoy it. For a child under 13, the same idea works on a board at home.

What does turning captures off do?

Landing on another colour's piece stops sending it back to base. Pieces simply share the square, and nobody loses ground to anybody. The lobby puts it in one short line: off means shared squares and no one sent back.

Two rules lose their point. Safe squares exist to protect against captures, so with none to fear they do nothing. A pair of one colour is shelter from capture, and with nothing to shelter from it does nothing too. Everything else carries on: the six that frees a piece, the exact number to finish and the 30-second clock.

Why do captures hurt young players most?

Because they are common. Any piece within six squares ahead of a rival is hit by a given throw 16.7% of the time. Only eight of the 52 squares are safe, so most places a piece can stand are open to attack.

Then there is the price. By my arithmetic a single piece needs close to 21 throws for the whole journey. Lose one at the halfway mark and ten of those go with it, plus the wait for another six. An adult shrugs. A younger player sees a long afternoon wiped away.

What does a game feel like without captures?

Calm. It turns into a pure race, where every decision is about getting a piece home and luck settles most of it. That is why it suits beginners: nobody is punished for a choice they did not yet understand.

It also levels the ages, which suits a family game night. A younger player and an adult stand on equal terms, because experience counts for little when nothing can be taken. The cost is tension. Without captures there is no moment when the room gasps.

What about extra throws?

With captures off, a capture can never earn a bonus. The extra-throws switch then matters in one place only, which is bringing a piece home. A six still throws again either way. For the shortest, simplest ludo game, switch extra throws off as well, so that only a six repeats.

How do you bring captures back?

Do it in steps, the way any skill is learnt. Each step is its own game, because the setting is locked once play begins.

  1. Start with captures off, and perhaps Quick Ludo, so the first game is short.
  2. Turn captures on and keep extra throws off. A capture hurts now but cannot snowball.
  3. Turn extra throws on. This is the standard setup.

Talk about what changed at each step. A short explanation of why safe squares matter lands better right after someone has lost a piece than before any piece was at risk.

What if the older players get bored?

They may. A race without captures is a thin game for an experienced player. One answer is two games in a row: the first for the youngest, the second with captures on for everyone. Another is to let the experienced players coach and keep their own pieces moving.

Where do you set it?

The switch sits in the lobby settings at Boardit's Ludo, and every seat can read it before the first throw. Teenagers and adults who want ludo with friends can use it for a gentler start, and it is the quickest way I know to make ludo online welcoming to a newcomer. Each switch has its own one-line note, so nobody has to learn the ludo rules twice.

A last thought

A house rule is a tool. Use it to keep the evening pleasant, and bring the standard version back when the table is ready for it.
