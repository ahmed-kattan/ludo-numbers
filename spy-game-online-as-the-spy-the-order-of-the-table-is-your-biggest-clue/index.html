Spy game online as the spy: the order of the table is your biggest clue

Ahmed Moaz · 31 October 2026

You are the spy. Every imposter game is a contest of information, and in this one everyone else holds a word while you hold a category. A spy game online also gives you something the card does not mention: the order of the table. Where you stand in a lap decides how much you have heard before you must speak. That is the one piece of information you can count.

What does the order tell you?

Clues go round in seat order, and the game shifts the starting seat by one place each round. In a lap, the player in seat k hears k minus 1 clues before speaking. The first speaker hears none. The last speaker in a table of six hears five.

The code comment says why it rotates: speaking first is the worst seat in the game, because you have no earlier clue to lean on while the spy has them all. The same rule makes first place the most dangerous seat for a spy. If it falls to you, the lap is a bluff with no cover.

What do you do from the first seat?

Playing with friends makes this seat feel worse, because they know your habits. You have the category, so use it as a floor. Give a clue that is true of almost anything in that category, and that a civilian might also give. It should be short, because a typed clue holds 20 characters at most.

The wrong move is to be clever. A bold guess about the word can be wildly wrong, and it will sit on the screen until the vote. A safe, general clue costs you nothing yet. The table will not catch you on the first lap with a clue like that, because every other clue is still to come.

What do you do from the last seat?

You have the most to work with. Read every clue before yours. Look for the one thing they all share. Then write a clue that repeats that shared quality without adding anything new.

Do not copy the clue just before yours. The civilians may have thought of it first, and an echo is the cheapest kind of tell. Pick the idea that three of them have touched on, and phrase it as your own.

Why does the second lap matter?

Because no vote can start before it. The first window to call a vote comes after two laps, and it needs a majority of the players. So you will hear every other player twice before the table is able to vote.

By then you have two chances to learn. After lap one, treat the clues as a puzzle: what single word fits all of them? After lap two, you can usually narrow your ideas to a handful.

Keep a short list in your head. If you can name three candidate words, make your next clue one that fits all three.

What about the 25-second clock?

It is your enemy if you hesitate. If it runs out, your slot reads "no answer", and a blank tells them something. Type a short clue early rather than a perfect one late. Two blank turns in a row also take you out of the game.

To get a feel for the order from the inside, Boardit's Who's the Spy is how the browser version seats three to eight people at one table.

What happens if the table catches you?

The round is not over for you. A lone spy is allowed a single try at naming the word, whether or not the table named them. The guess is a pick from ten words, and a blind pick has a one-in-ten chance. A spy who has kept notes through two laps is not blind. That is where the order helps most: the more clues you heard, the shorter your list.

A correct guess earns a point. It also stands alone from the vote, so a spy who is ejected can still finish with something.

The seat you do not choose

You cannot pick your place in the order. Your seat decides it, and the game rotates it. What you can choose is how to use it. Early, hide in the category. Late, borrow what the table has already built. In both seats, send something before the clock runs out.
