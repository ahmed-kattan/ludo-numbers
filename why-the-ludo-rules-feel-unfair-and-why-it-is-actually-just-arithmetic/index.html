Why the ludo rules feel unfair, and why it is actually just arithmetic

Ahmed Moaz · 24 October 2026

Nearly everyone who plays this ludo game has said, at some point, "this game is rigged". You wait ten turns for a six, an opponent throws three in a row, and the piece you were bringing home is captured by a lucky throw. It feels unfair. But the ludo rules are the same for everyone, and when you look at the numbers, the unfairness is mostly the normal spread of chance. Here are the main sources, with figures.

The wait for a six

Nothing leaves base until a six arrives. The chance of throwing no six in three throws is 57.9%, so more than half of players wait through three turns. In six throws it is 33.5%, and in ten throws, 16.2%. One player in six will sit through ten turns with nothing out. That is not a curse; it is what a one-in-six chance looks like.

The speed of the journey

A single piece needs about 20.8 throws to get home, but the spread is wide. Only 12.9% of journeys are done in 15 throws, 59.2% in 20, and 93.4% in 30. So two pieces that start together can finish fifteen throws apart. Over four pieces, the differences average out a little, but they never disappear.

The exact finish

At the end, a piece within six squares of home needs one particular number. Each throw has a one in six chance, so the average wait is six throws whatever the distance. Some players wait two turns, some fifteen. A piece that sits waiting in plain sight feels like a personal insult.

Captures

A piece one to six squares ahead of an enemy is hit with chance one in six per throw. Over a long journey a piece faces several such moments, so being captured at least once is likely. A capture also gives the capturer an extra throw, so luck compounds: the lucky player gains, the unlucky one loses.

Bonus throws

A six, a capture and a piece home each buy another go. The same player can therefore take several moves in a row, while others sit and watch. It is the main reason a game can feel as if one person is playing alone.

Why does it feel so personal?

Because Ludo is the kind of game in which you can see the disaster coming. You watch a rival's piece line up behind yours, count the squares and hope. That slow dread is more painful than a surprise.

Is it really fair?

Yes, in the sense that the die is the same for everyone and the rules apply equally. Over many games, each seat wins about as often as any other. In a single game, luck decides a lot. That mix is what the game offers: not equal results, but equal chances.

What can you do about it?

  • Remember that the average player waits six throws for a six.
  • Use safe squares and pairs to give yourself protection.
  • Keep more than one piece moving, so exact finishes do not freeze you.
  • Treat a bad run as a story to tell later.
  • Play several games in one evening, so luck evens out.

Where can you check it yourself?

The free table at Boardit, which I build, applies the ludo rules without bias, so you can play ludo online, and ludo with friends in different cities, and watch the pattern across a few games. Free play means you can try it as often as you like. It seats two to four, and you can keep a tally of the longest wait for a six. Most groups find the unluckiest player waited longer than the mean, and the luckiest shorter, as the numbers predict.

A final thought

Games that feel unfair often are the ones people remember. Nobody retells the game in which all went as expected. The six that came after ten tries, or the capture on the last square, stays in the family for years.
