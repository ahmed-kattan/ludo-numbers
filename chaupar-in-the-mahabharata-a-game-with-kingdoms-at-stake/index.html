Chaupar in the Mahabharata: a game with kingdoms at stake

Ahmed Moaz · 16 October 2026

Ludo online today is thirty to sixty minutes of dice and colour, with nothing at stake but a turn. Its distant relatives are older and heavier. In the Mahabharata, a game of dice at a royal court costs a king his kingdom, then his brothers, then himself. This piece follows that thread: what the epic's dice match staked, how sure anyone can be that the game was chaupar, and which of today's ludo rules still carry its marks. It is not light reading, and I have kept to the stakes rather than the details.

The dice match, and what was staked

The match sits in the Sabha Parva, the book of the assembly hall. Its first part is the Dyuta Parva. Yudhishthira, the eldest Pandava, plays Shakuni, who plays for Duryodhana, the Pandavas' cousin and rival. Shakuni is Duryodhana's maternal uncle, and in the usual telling his plan rests on one weakness: Yudhishthira's fondness for dice.

Look at the order of the stakes, because it is the shape of the story. He stakes his kingdom and loses. He stakes his brothers and loses. He stakes himself and loses. Last he stakes Draupadi, his wife, and the epic turns on that moment. In the summary I read, the assembly then rules that last stake invalid and the losses are returned. A second match follows, in the Anudyuta Parva, with a single stake that decides it. The loser goes into exile for twelve years and spends a thirteenth unrecognised, and Yudhishthira loses.

Each stake is raised after a loss. That is the grim engine of the episode: a player chasing what he has just lost.

Was it chaupar?

Possibly, and it is not proven. Wikipedia's article on chaupar says the Mahabharata's dice match is claimed to be a form of the game, and presents it as a claim. The article on pachisi, chaupar's close relative, says the episode is often assumed to be pachisi but reports that Irving Finkel says the descriptions do not fit. The chaupar article itself carries a notice that it needs more citations.

What the sources do agree on is the family. Chaupar is a cross-and-circle game played with cowrie shells, seven in the main method, or in some versions with long four-sided dice. Pachisi, which Wikipedia says originated from chaupar, throws six cowries, or seven in some versions. The epic's game may be a cousin of these, or something else that used dice. Both readings are possible, and I would not pick one for you.

What the board looked like

Chaupar was played on a cloth board in the shape of a cross. Each arm is three columns of eight squares, according to Wikipedia. Players race their pieces round the arms and home to the centre. If you have seen a Ludo board, the outline is familiar: a cross, four arms, a path that runs out and back. Counting squares on the arms gives 4 x 24 = 96, my multiplication of Wikipedia's figures. Boardit's Ludo path is smaller: 52 shared squares and a five-square private column for each of four colours, 72 in all.

Ludo came much later. Wikipedia says it was marketed in England in 1896 and shares characteristics with pachisi and other cross-and-circle games. Boardit's own FAQ gives the name's meaning as Latin for "I play". It is a simplification: fewer ways to throw, a printed board in place of the embroidered cloth.

Cowries are not a die

A cowrie throw works differently from a die. Suppose, as an idealised case, that each of seven shells lands open-side up half the time. The number showing open is then 0 to 7, and the chances are 1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7 and 1 out of 128. Three or four open comes up 70 times in 128, about 55 per cent, while none or all seven comes up twice in 128, about 1.6 per cent. A die gives every number one chance in six.

Real shells are not fair coins, and Wikipedia's pachisi article gives its own odds, so treat this as the shape and not the figures. The shape is the point: cowrie throws cluster in the middle, and the extreme throw is a rarity. A rule that needs one particular number would feel very different on a die, where the gate opens one throw in six, than on a throw that rarely lands on an end.

Is Ludo really descended from chaupar?

Probably, by way of pachisi. Wikipedia says pachisi originated from chaupar and lists Ludo among the games derived from it. Ludo keeps the cross-shaped race but swaps cowries for one die and adds a six to open the gate. No source I opened traces the line step by step.

The ludo rules that survived, and what changed

Four marks of the family are still on a Ludo table. The pieces race round a cross. Pieces can be sent back. The centre must be reached, and on this board it must be reached exactly. And the throw decides what you may do, while you decide which piece does it.

What changed is the stake. Take Boardit's Ludo board, with four pieces each. After a six lets a piece in, it walks 56 squares to the centre, so a full journey is 224 squares. A capture sends one piece back to the corner, and the furthest it can reach is the 50th square, because from the 51st the piece is in its own private column where no rival can land. So the worst single capture costs 50 squares of 224, about 22 per cent of a player's whole journey, and a pair or a starred square would have protected it.

That is a bounded stake. Nothing in the rules lets one throw take everything, and the rules say so plainly. The epic's table had no such limit. It raised the stake with every loss, and the whole episode follows from that.

What the dice decide

In the epic's match, the dice decide who wins each round, and the only real decision belongs to the player: whether to raise the stake. That is a gambling game in the plainest sense, and the episode turns on that choosing.

At a Ludo table the dice decide something smaller. They tell you which numbers you may play, and you choose which piece plays them. The choice is about tempo and risk, never about what else to put on the table. That is my reading of why the two feel so different, and it is a view of the rules, not a claim about the epic's authors.

Playing ludo with friends, and keeping the stakes at zero

A modern table of ludo with friends is a gentler place. Bring a friend, a die and a board, and agree the rules of Ludo before the first throw. Winning earns a laugh, not a kingdom. Three things at the table are worth noticing:

  1. The throw still sets what you may do, as the shells once did.
  2. A capture still costs you the ground, not the game.
  3. The centre still wants the exact number.

If you want to read the episode itself, start with a full translation of the Sabha Parva and read the dice match with the chapters around it, not from a summary like the ones I used here.
