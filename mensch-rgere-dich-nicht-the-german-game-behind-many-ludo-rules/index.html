Mensch ärgere Dich nicht: the German game behind many Ludo rules

Ahmed Moaz · 18 October 2026

If you are German, Austrian or Swiss, you may have grown up with a game that looks a lot like Ludo and has a name you can hardly pronounce if you are not: Mensch ärgere Dich nicht. It means roughly "man, don't get angry", and it has sold in the tens of millions. It also shaped the ludo rules many families use today. Here is what the encyclopedia sources say about it, and how it compares.

Who invented it?

Josef Friedrich Schmidt, a man from Amberg, developed the game in the winter of 1907 to 1908 in a workshop in Munich-Giesing. He went on to found a games company, Schmidt Spiele. The game was first published in 1910, and series production began in 1914. By the sources I read, about 70 million copies have been sold since 1914, and around a million of those by 1920.

One popular story says the game was given out to soldiers in field hospitals during the First World War. The page I read marks that claim as unverified, so I leave it out.

Why that name?

The name comes from the rule everybody remembers: when your piece lands on an opponent's, the opponent's piece goes back to the start. A player who has been sent back, after a long walk round the board, is expected to feel angry. The game's title tells them not to.

How is it like Ludo?

Both come from Pachisi. Each has a cross-shaped board, four players, four pieces each and a single die. A piece goes round the track and into a home area. A capture sends a piece back. Schmidt, according to the source, removed the symbolism of Pachisi and kept the simple race.

How is it different?

Two rules stand out in the German game:

  1. A piece on the start field cannot be knocked out.
  2. A player must leave the start field as soon as they can, so you cannot hold a piece back.

A common variation, which the source calls a variation and not a base rule, gives a player three tries to throw a six when all pieces are in the base. Many families use that to speed up the start.

How do the Ludo rules I use compare?

In the version I run, a single throw is allowed per turn and a six is needed to leave base. A six, a capture or a piece brought home earns another throw, and a third six in a row loses the turn. Two pieces on a square protect each other, and eight marked squares are safe. The exact number is needed to come home. Each rule has a cousin in the German game, but the details differ, so check which set your table is using.

Which should you play?

If you like a quicker start, try the three-tries variation. If you like the first six to feel earned, play the single-throw version. Both are legitimate. The board and the pieces are the same, so a family can try each on different nights.

Where can you play ludo online?

You can play ludo with friends or family, from two players up to four, and the table enforces the rules so nobody has to remember them. It is a small way to share a game that has crossed more than a century.

A note for German-speaking players

If your family calls the game by its German name and plays with its own house rules, those rules are worth keeping. A game that lasts over a hundred years survives through families' own versions.

What remains uncertain

I rely on one page for the history and have not checked every regional rule. If your region plays differently, trust your own board.
