Imposter game vocabulary: twelve terms new players ask about

Ahmed Moaz · 8 November 2026

Every imposter game has a small language, and newcomers meet it all at once. What is a lap? What is a call? Is a runoff the same as a tie? Here are twelve terms as they are used in Who's the Spy, the game I build, with the number or rule that goes with each. Where another version uses a word differently, I say so.

Spy

The player who does not know the word. They are told only the category. The host chooses how many spies there are, from one up to half the table.

Civilian

Any player who knows the word. In some versions they are called villagers or insiders. Their job is to prove they know, without helping the spy.

Category

The family the word belongs to, such as food or sports. There are 15 in the game. The spy sees the category; everyone else sees the word.

Clue

A short hint about the word, given on your turn. Online, a clue is typed, up to 20 characters, and you have 25 seconds. On one phone, it is spoken.

Lap

One full round of clues, with every player speaking once. A vote can be requested after every two laps.

Call window

The ten seconds after every two laps in which anyone may press for a vote. The vote begins only if a majority of the players ask. Otherwise the clues continue.

Vote

Each player names who they think the spy is, within 45 seconds. You cannot vote for yourself.

Runoff

A second vote, between only the tied players, when the first ends level. If that ends level too, nobody is out.

Tally

The short moment, about seven seconds, when the votes are shown. It is the table's first look at the result.

Reveal

The moment the word is shown to everyone. It lasts ten seconds. It is the part of the round players like most.

Guess

A caught lone spy's last chance: a choice from ten words, with 30 seconds. A right guess earns a point.

Round

A deal, the clue laps, a vote and the reveal. The game has three by default. Nobody sits out: the next round deals every player a fresh card.

Can your group invent its own words?

Yes, and it is worth doing. Every group that plays for a few weeks starts to name the moments that keep happening: the clue that gave it away, the spy who was never suspected, the vote that ended in a tie. Giving them names turns a series of games into a shared history. Write the names on a card, and a newcomer can read the group's history in a minute.

The official terms above are for the game; the invented ones are for the friends who play it.

Which terms confuse newcomers?

Lap, call and runoff. Lap sounds like a race. Call sounds like a phone. Runoff sounds like an escape. Explain them with an example in the first round and the confusion disappears.

How do the terms fit together in one round?

A round begins with a deal, and the spy sees the category. Players give clues in laps. After two laps the call window opens, and if more than half ask, the vote begins. The tally shows the result, level votes send the leaders to a runoff, and the caught lone spy takes the guess. Then comes the reveal, and the next round starts. Reading the terms in that order is the quickest way to learn the structure of the game, and it fits on the back of a coaster.

Where can you hear them in use?

To hear these terms in use, open a table of Who's the Spy on Boardit: a spy game online that also works as an imposter game online with friends, free for three to eight players. The screen names each stage as it happens, so the vocabulary arrives in context.

A last tip for teaching

Teach four words first: spy, clue, vote, reveal. The rest arrive when they matter.
