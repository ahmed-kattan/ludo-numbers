Imposter game online or on one phone: which format suits your table?

Ahmed Moaz · 28 October 2026

Who's the Spy can be played two ways. Everyone can join from their own device, or one phone can be passed around a table. The word-and-clue game inside is the same either way. The experience is not. If you are choosing between an imposter game online and a shared phone for tonight, this is what actually differs.

What is the difference, side by side?

Own phones, onlineOne shared phone
Players3 to 83 to 12
Who needs the siteEveryoneOnly the host
CluesTyped, up to 20 charactersSpoken aloud
Turn clock25 secondsNone
Calling a voteMore than half ask, in a 10-second window after every two lapsThe host decides
The cardShows for 10 seconds, then the round startsWaits until each person has seen theirs

The engine behind both is the same, which is why the scoring and the spy draw are identical.

When does the online format win?

Your table is spread out. Maybe you are on a call, in different rooms, or in different cities. Everyone needs their own screen, and that is the only way to keep a word private from the others.

It also wins when you want clues written down. A typed clue is exact, with no mumbling and no talking over each other. And if you have a shy player, typing takes the performance out of it.

The clock helps too. Twenty-five seconds per clue keeps the round moving without a referee.

When does one phone win?

You are in one room and not everyone has a device with them. A child can play without a device of their own, and so can a grandparent. Only the host needs the site open.

It also holds more people. A party of ten or twelve does not fit an online table, which stops at eight, but it fits one phone.

Spoken clues bring different pleasures. You see faces, hear hesitation and catch the spy's pause. Some groups find that more fun than the typed version.

What does passing the phone cost you?

Privacy, partly. Each person looks at their card, taps to hide it and hands the phone on. The card does not move itself away, because the game waits for a person. That works, but a nosy neighbour can lean over a shoulder. Hold the phone close and keep the group a little apart.

There is also no turn clock on one phone, in the engine I read. People talk at their own pace, which is good for a relaxed table and a poor fit if you have a train to catch.

And the host has the extra job of deciding when the table has talked enough. That is a real decision in a game that depends on it, so choose a host who will not cut it short or let it run on.

Which should you pick for a mixed group?

Think of who is in the room.

  • All in one place, with ten or fewer people: one phone is simple.
  • Everybody has a phone and the group is eight or fewer: online is smoother, and it gives you written clues.
  • Some in the room, some remote: online, with the people in the room each using their own device.

If you are still unsure, start with whichever format needs the fewest explanations. You can change the next time.

How do you start either one?

Boardit's Who's the Spy lets the host pick a shared phone or separate devices when setting up the table. As a spy game online it asks nothing of guests except a name.

A rule of thumb for the first night

If more than half the people know each other well, choose spoken clues on one phone. If they do not, choose typed clues on their own phones, where nobody has to perform.
