Draw and guess with 12 players: how to run the biggest table

Ahmed Moaz · 25 October 2026

A draw and guess game scales better than most. Twelve people can sit at one table, and it still works, because everyone is busy guessing eleven drawings out of twelve. But a big table has its own problems: a long evening, a noisy chat and a scoring system that behaves differently when ten people can guess at once. Here is how to run it.

How big is the table?

The maximum is twelve players. The default table size is eight, and the host raises it in the lobby. Everyone draws once per round, so with the default three rounds a table of twelve plays 36 drawings.

How long will that take?

At the default 100 seconds, the ceiling is 36 times 100 seconds, which is 60 minutes of drawing, before word choices and reveals. Most drawings end early because everyone has guessed, so the real time is shorter. For a party, that is a good evening. For a quick game, cut the rounds to two and the time to 60 seconds, which is 24 drawings and 24 minutes at most.

How does scoring behave with so many guessers?

The order share matters most here. Each correct guess is multiplied by a share that falls with the number who guessed before: about 56% for the second, 38% for the third, 29% for the fourth, 24% for the fifth, and never below 20%. In a table of twelve, the later guessers all receive the floor of 20% of their speed points.

So the first few guessers score well and the rest score little. The race to be first is fierce. That rewards quick readers and punishes people who wait to be sure. It also makes the drawer's job important: a drawing that many people read quickly pays the drawer the average of their speed points.

How do you keep a large table together?

  1. Choose a short drawing time, 60 seconds, so the pencil moves quickly.
  2. Keep hints at two, so most drawings finish.
  3. Agree that guesses are shouted as well as typed, if you are in a room together.
  4. Let the pencil pass in the same order each round, so nobody wonders when it is their turn.

What can go wrong?

  • The chat scrolls too fast to read. Ask people to keep guesses short.
  • A few people dominate. Cap it by agreeing that anyone who has guessed right stays quiet.
  • Latecomers. Start the game when most are there and add a short break before round two.
  • Fatigue. Take a five-minute break after round two.

What about splitting into two tables?

If the group is loud or the evening is short, two tables of six work well, and the winners can meet in a final. Each table plays faster, with 18 drawings at most, about 24 minutes. You lose the chaos of twelve but gain the chance to talk.

Where can you set up a big table?

At Board It, a free site where you can play a drawing game online with friends, the host raises the table size to twelve in the lobby. It runs in a browser on phones and laptops, a party game that needs nothing downloaded, and guests join with a link.

What should the host do before the game?

Check the list of players, remove anyone who joined by mistake, and confirm the rules aloud. Tell people the plan: two or three rounds, about 40 minutes, with a break in the middle. Appoint a helper to watch the chat for people who need help, and to remind quiet players that a wrong guess is free.

It also helps to run a one-drawing practice round with a silly word before the game starts. Everyone sees how the points work, and the nervous ones get over their first drawing before it counts.

How do you handle the scoreboard?

With twelve names, the scoreboard becomes the second show. Read it aloud between rounds. Celebrate the climber of the round, the player who gained most places. Players in the middle of the table are often the most excited, since twelve players mean twelve chances to move up.

A closing thought

A big table is a performance. Someone will draw something so strange that eleven people shout the same wrong answer at once. That moment is the reason to invite everyone.
