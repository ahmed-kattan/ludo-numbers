A trade in Property Rush: how to make an offer people will accept

Ahmed Moaz · 11 November 2026

Trading is where Property Rush is won and lost. A trade can complete a set for you and break one for your rival. This is a short guide to offers.

What a trade is

You propose to swap properties, money or both with another player. They accept, refuse or counter. The bots make offers to people too, and those offers are meant to be fair.

Why trade at all

If you own every city in a country, the rent doubles even before you build. So the city you are missing is worth far more to you than its price. The player who has it is worth more to you still.

Here are the costs of owning a whole country, from the game's code: the UAE 130, Egypt 330, Italy 430, France 570, Japan 670, Canada 760, Germany 810 and the Netherlands 910. The price of the missing city is a fraction of that. The doubled rent on the others is the prize.

A good offer

  1. Offer them something that completes their set. A trade that helps both of you is easier to accept.
  2. Pay in cash, not in debt. While you owe money you cannot roll.
  3. Do not hand over a set. A trade that gives away a complete country gives away doubled rent.
  4. Ask for a city that breaks a rival's set. It is a double win.

What to avoid

Do not offer a mortgaged city as if it were whole. A mortgaged city collects no rent, and lifting the mortgage costs the loan plus about 10%.

When to refuse

If the trade gives your rival a complete country, say no, however much cash is on the table.

Where to play

Boardit has Property Rush, an independent Monopoly alternative for two to six players, free in the browser.

A set worth offering

Offers work best when they complete a set for someone. Germany costs 810 in all: Hamburg 260, Munich 270 and Berlin 280. A player who holds two of the three cities has a reason to pay a premium for the third, because the full set doubles the rent before any house goes up.

A last word

A trade is only fair if both sides gain. A trade that costs you a set is never fair.
