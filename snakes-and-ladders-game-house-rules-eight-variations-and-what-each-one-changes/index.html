Snakes and ladders game house rules: eight variations and what each one changes

Ahmed Moaz · 26 October 2026

No two families play the snakes and ladders game quite the same way. One gives a sixth throw on a six, another bounces you back from the end. These are not mistakes; they are house rules, and they shape the evening. Here are eight that families commonly use, what each one does to the length and mood, and the one rule where I can give you a number.

What is the standard version?

For reference, the version I run is strict: a single throw each turn, nothing extra for a six, an exact number to finish, and a half-minute turn timer. Everything below is a change from that.

Which house rules change the length?

  1. Exact finish off. A counter that throws more than it needs simply wins. The average journey drops from 39.2 to 35.8 throws, so the rule costs 3.4 throws of waiting.
  2. Bounce back. If you overshoot, you move the surplus backwards from 100. It keeps people moving, but it can drag the end out when a counter ping-pongs between 97 and 99.
  3. Extra throw on a six. This speeds up the game and gives sixes a small thrill. It also makes the swing at the end bigger.
  4. Start on a six. Counters wait off the board until a six arrives. It delays the start by six throws on average, because a six turns up once in every six throws.

Which house rules change the mood?

  1. Capture. Landing on a rival sends them back to the start. It turns a harmless game into a sharper one, which some families love and some find upsetting. Younger children often dislike it.
  2. Team play. Players share a counter in pairs and take turns throwing. It suits mixed ages, because an adult can help a child count.
  3. Forfeits. A player who lands on a snake performs a small silly task. A good rule for adults and teenagers, but it should be optional.
  4. Choose your direction. When a die shows a six, the player may go backwards instead. This is the only rule here that adds a decision. It can turn a luck game into a puzzle, and it takes a little thought.

How should you pick one?

Start with the youngest player. If they get upset by a loss, avoid capture. If they get bored waiting, add the extra throw on a six. If the adults get restless, try forfeits or team play.

Change one rule at a time, and play a full game before you decide. Two changes together hide which one made the difference.

Does any rule make the game fairer?

The first player has a tiny edge: 50.8% of two-player games in my calculation. A neat house rule is to let the loser of the previous game start the next. It costs nothing and gently evens things out.

Can you play house rules online?

Most online tables use the standard rules and do not let you change them, which is a good reason to keep the printed board for the house-rule evenings. For snakes and ladders online with friends, the free table at Board It has only the standard version, so a family can use it as the baseline for two players or eight and add its own agreement on top, for example forfeits done on camera while the multiplayer game runs.

A note on writing them down

Families forget their house rules. Write yours on a card and keep it in the box. When a cousin visits and asks, the card ends the argument in a second.

Where the numbers stop

Only one of these rules has a measured effect in this article, and it is the exact-finish rule. The others are described from how the game is built. If you test one and get a clear result, trust yours over mine.
