Strategy board games online: when to mortgage a city, and when it is a mistake

Ahmed Moaz · 2 November 2026

In strategy board games online, a loan is a decision you make with cash on a clock. Property Rush, a property trading game I build, lets you borrow against any city you own, and the temptation is to treat that as a panic button. It is better as a tool. Here is how I think about it, with the exact sums the game uses, so you can decide before the rent arrives.

What does a mortgage do?

Mortgaging a city hands you half its price in cash. The city then collects no rent until you lift the mortgage, and lifting it costs what you raised plus a tenth, rounded up. These are the real sums for six cities.

Two rules shape when you can do it. You may mortgage on your own turn, or at any moment your cash is below zero. And every building in the country must be sold first, each house returning half its cost: a Giza house that cost 50 sells for 25.

Why is a bare city cheap to mortgage?

Because what you give up is small. Take Giza. Its rent on bare land is 6, and lifting costs 6 more than you raised. In my simulation of 3,000,000 turns of one token, Egypt's three cities took 6.8% of all stops, so Giza gets roughly 2.3% of them. Multiply that by the rent and you lose about 0.14 a turn per rival. The extra 6 you pay at the end equals about forty-four rival turns of that missing rent.

Toronto tells the opposite story. It takes 2.73% of stops and charges 50 bare, so a mortgaged Toronto forgoes about 1.4 per rival turn, ten times Giza's loss. Over thirty rival turns, a mortgaged Giza costs about 10 in premium and silent rent, and a mortgaged Toronto about 62. The dearer the rent, the more each quiet turn costs.

Which city should you mortgage first?

Order your cities by how little they earn you and how little they matter. My order:

  1. A bare city in a country you will never finish. It is a lone deed, and a rival who needs it will pay more than its mortgage value anyway.
  2. A bare city in a quiet corner of the board, where traffic and rent are both low.
  3. An airport or utility you hold alone, if its rent is small.

The mortgage value is a floor for the price, not the price. If a rival would complete a country with your city, sell it to them for far more instead of borrowing against it.

Which city should you never mortgage?

Not the one that finishes your own country. A mortgaged city stops its neighbours from taking houses, because no building is allowed in a country while any city of it is mortgaged. You would be paying to freeze your own plan.

Not a city with houses, either, until you have counted the cost. Selling down loses half of what you spent and every rung of rent you had built. Nearly always it is cheaper to mortgage bare cities in another country first.

What should you do when the rent is already due?

Your cash falls below zero and the game stops you from rolling until you climb back above it. That is the moment mortgaging was designed for. You can mortgage, sell or trade, and Boardit lets you do all three while you are in the red. Take the cheapest source first: a bare lone city, then a swap, and only then houses.

Remember the host's switches. Mortgaging can be turned off at the start, and then debt is far harsher, because only sales and trades remain.

When should you lift it?

When you have cash to cover the lift and also the next rent you can foresee. A mortgage lifted too early leaves you broke again. A mortgage lifted too late keeps a city silent while rivals walk past it. If you plan to trade the city, think about lifting it first, because the person who receives it inherits the bill.

What is the short version?

Treat the mortgage as a cheap loan on cities that do little, and an expensive one on cities that do a lot. Property Rush is an independent Monopoly alternative, and its loan rules reward a player who counts before the dice fall. Try them in a trading game online with friends, and keep a note of what each mortgage really cost you.
